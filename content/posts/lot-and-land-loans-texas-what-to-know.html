A lot or land loan finances the purchase of land without a finished home on it. Lenders treat land as riskier than a home, so these loans usually require more money down and come with different terms than a standard mortgage. Improved residential lots with road access and utilities are generally the easiest to finance. Raw land without utilities or access is harder. Your plans for building, and how soon, also shape your options.

Key takeaways

  • Improved lots are generally easier to finance than raw land.
  • Land loans usually require more money down than home loans.
  • Utilities, access, survey, flood zone and restrictions all affect eligibility.
  • If you plan to build soon, a construction-to-permanent loan may cover the lot and the build together.
  • Texas MUD and PID taxes can apply to land and change once a home is built.

What is the difference between improved and raw land?

An improved lot has utilities available and legal road access, often in a platted subdivision. Raw land may have none of those. Lenders can more easily value improved lots and see a clear path to a home being built, so they generally offer more options for them.

What do lenders check on a lot or land purchase?

  • Survey, legal description and access
  • Utilities: water or well, sewer or septic, electricity
  • Flood zone and drainage
  • Zoning, deed restrictions and HOA rules
  • Property taxes, including MUD or PID assessments
  • Title issues, such as easements or mineral rights reservations

Should you buy the lot first or finance it with the build?

If you have found the land but are not ready to build, a lot loan secures it now and your equity can later go toward the construction loan. If you are ready to build within a short period, a construction-to-permanent loan that includes the lot purchase can mean one loan and one closing. Some buyers pay cash for the land and use its value toward the construction loan's down payment.
