TL;DR

A price cut lowers the loan amount; a rate buydown lowers the buyer's rate and monthly payment. For payment-sensitive buyers a buydown often does more per dollar and protects the builder's comparable sales.

A price cut and a rate buydown can cost a builder the same amount, but they do very different things for the buyer. A price cut lowers the sales price, the loan amount and the appraised comparable for the next sale. A rate buydown uses the same dollars to lower the buyer's interest rate, either for the first years of the loan or for its full life, which usually has a bigger effect on the monthly payment. The right choice depends on the buyer, the loan program and what you need your comparable sales to show.

Key takeaways

  • A price cut lowers the price and the loan amount, and resets your comparable sales.
  • A buydown spends the same dollars on the buyer's rate, which usually moves the monthly payment more.
  • Temporary buydowns lower the payment for the first years; permanent buydowns lower it for the life of the loan.
  • Every loan program caps how much a seller can contribute, so the incentive has to fit the buyer's loan.
  • The best incentive is the one that solves the specific buyer's problem: payment, cash to close or qualifying.

What does a price cut actually do?

A price cut lowers the contract price and loan amount, which reduces the buyer's payment slightly and the cash needed at closing, but it can lower future comparable sales.

A price cut reduces the contract price. The buyer borrows less, pays a little less each month and may need slightly less for the down payment. For the builder, the lower price becomes a comparable sale that appraisers and future buyers will see, which can pull down values across the rest of the community.

What does a rate buydown do?

A rate buydown uses builder dollars to lower the buyer's interest rate, which usually lowers the monthly payment more than a price cut of the same cost.

With a buydown, the builder pays the lender to reduce the buyer's interest rate. A permanent buydown uses discount points to lower the rate for the life of the loan. A temporary buydown lowers the payment for the first year or two, with the difference funded up front, then the payment steps up to the full note rate. Because the dollars go toward the payment instead of the price, buyers often feel the benefit more, and your sales price stays intact.

Which incentive fits which buyer?

Rate buydowns usually help payment-sensitive buyers most, while price cuts or closing-cost help suit buyers limited by loan amount or cash to close.

  • Payment-sensitive buyers usually benefit most from a buydown.
  • Buyers short on cash to close may be better served by closing-cost help, within program limits.
  • Buyers who barely qualify need care: with many temporary buydowns, the buyer still qualifies at the full note rate, so the buydown does not change approval.
  • Buyers planning to refinance or move soon may get more from a temporary buydown or closing-cost help than a permanent buydown.

How much can a builder contribute?

Every loan program caps seller and builder contributions, and the cap depends on the program, the buyer's down payment and occupancy.

Every loan program limits seller and builder contributions, sometimes called interested party contributions. The limit depends on the loan type, the buyer's down payment and how the home will be used. Money spent beyond the limit can force a price reduction late in the process. See how seller-concession limits work by loan type.

How should builders decide which incentive to offer?

Decide community by community, based on your buyers' price range, how payment-sensitive they are, appraisal risk and each program's concession limits.

  1. Look at who is actually buying in that community and what is stopping them: payment, cash or qualifying.
  2. Compare what the same incentive budget does as a price cut, a temporary buydown and a permanent buydown.
  3. Check each option against program contribution limits for your typical buyer.
  4. Advertise carefully. Promoting a specific rate or payment in your marketing triggers federal disclosure requirements, so involve your lender before you print it.
