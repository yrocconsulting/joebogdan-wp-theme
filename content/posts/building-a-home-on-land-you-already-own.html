TL;DR

If you already own your lot, its equity can often count toward the down payment on a construction-to-permanent loan. Lenders use the land's appraised value minus what you owe, and utilities, access and restrictions still matter.

If you already own your lot, the equity in that land can often be applied toward the down payment on a construction-to-permanent loan. The lender uses the land's value, based on the appraisal, and subtracts anything you still owe on it. If you have a lot loan, the construction loan typically pays it off at closing. The rest of the process works like any construction loan: an approved builder, plans and a budget, an appraisal from plans and draws during the build.

Key takeaways

  • Land equity can often count toward your down payment.
  • Lenders typically use the appraised value of the land, minus what you owe on it.
  • An existing lot loan is usually paid off by the construction loan at closing.
  • Utilities, access, survey and restrictions still need to support the home you plan to build.
  • Owning the land outright can reduce the cash you need at closing.

How do lenders value land you already own?

Lenders typically use the land's appraised value, minus anything you owe on it, and may consider how long you have owned it and what you paid.

The appraisal values the finished home on the lot, and the lot itself is part of that value. How the land's value is credited toward your down payment can depend on how long you have owned it and what you paid, so bring your purchase documents and any loan statements to your first conversation.

What should you check before you start?

Check utilities, road access, the survey and flood zone, deed restrictions and HOA rules, and the property taxes that will apply once the home is built.

  • Utilities: water or well, sewer or septic, and electricity
  • Access: legal road access to the lot
  • Survey and flood zone: where the home can sit and whether flood insurance is needed
  • Restrictions: deed restrictions, HOA rules and minimum home sizes
  • Taxes: county, school, MUD or PID taxes once the home is built

What if the land is part of a larger tract?

With acreage or multiple parcels, the lender may count only the land tied to the home site, and rural comparables shape the appraisal.

Acreage, multiple parcels or land with agricultural use adds steps. The lender may only count the portion of land tied to the home site, and the appraisal will consider comparable rural properties. Talk through these early so the plans and budget match what can be financed.
