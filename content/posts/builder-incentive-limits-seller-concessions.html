TL;DR

Seller-concession limits depend on the buyer's loan program, down payment and occupancy. Builder contributions above the limit usually cannot be absorbed, so plan incentives inside each program's cap from the start.

Builder incentives are limited by the buyer's loan program. Conventional loans allow builder and seller contributions that range from 3% to 9% of the price, depending on the buyer's down payment and occupancy. FHA generally allows up to 6%. VA limits certain concessions to 4%, while some normal closing costs do not count toward that cap. Contributions above the limit usually have to be removed or turned into a price reduction, which is why incentives should be planned with the lender before the contract is signed.

Key takeaways

  • Contribution limits depend on the loan type, the down payment and how the home will be used.
  • Investment-property loans have tighter limits than primary-residence loans.
  • Money over the limit typically becomes a required price reduction.
  • Incentives can pay closing costs, prepaids and buydowns, but generally cannot be paid to the buyer as cash.
  • Program rules change, so confirm limits on each file before you promise an incentive.

What counts as a builder contribution?

Interested party contributions are costs paid by anyone with a financial interest in the sale, such as the builder or seller, toward the buyer's closing costs, prepaid items or rate buydowns.

Interested party contributions are costs paid by anyone with a financial interest in the sale, including the builder, the seller and the listing agent. They commonly include closing costs, prepaid items such as escrow deposits, and rate buydowns. Design-center credits and upgrades that become part of the home are generally treated differently from money applied to the buyer's financing costs, which is one reason to put incentives in writing clearly.

How do concession limits work by loan type?

Conventional, FHA and VA loans each set their own limits on seller and builder contributions, and conventional limits vary with the buyer's down payment and occupancy.

  • Conventional: limits range from 3% to 9% of the price for a primary residence, depending on the buyer's down payment, with a lower limit for investment properties.
  • FHA: generally up to 6% of the sales price toward eligible costs.
  • VA: concessions such as paying the buyer's debts or prepaid items are limited to 4% of the reasonable value, while normal closing costs and discount points are not counted toward that cap.
  • Jumbo and Non-QM: limits are set by each investor and can be lower, so check early.

What happens if an incentive exceeds the limit?

Contributions above the limit usually cannot simply be absorbed; they are typically treated as sales concessions that reduce the value used for the loan.

The excess usually cannot simply be absorbed. It is typically treated as a sales concession that reduces the price used for the loan, which can change the loan amount, the down payment and sometimes the buyer's approval, often late in the process. Planning the incentive against the buyer's program at contract avoids that.

How can a sales team apply concession limits consistently?

Check each buyer's loan program and down payment before structuring incentives, and confirm the final numbers with the lender before contracts are signed.

  1. Identify the buyer's likely loan program at the first meeting.
  2. Write incentives into the contract with specific uses: closing costs, prepaids or a buydown.
  3. Have the lender confirm the contribution fits the program before the contract is final.
  4. Keep design-center credits separate and clearly documented.
