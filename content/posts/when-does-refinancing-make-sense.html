Refinancing makes sense when what you gain - a lower payment, a shorter term, removing mortgage insurance or accessing equity - is worth more than what it costs, within the time you expect to keep the loan. The simplest test is your break-even point: total closing costs divided by your monthly savings tells you how many months it takes to come out ahead.

Key takeaways

  • Break-even months = closing costs ÷ monthly savings.
  • If you will sell or refinance again before the break-even point, it may not be worth it.
  • Cash-out refinancing on a Texas homestead follows specific state rules.
  • Sometimes the best advice is to wait. Joseph will tell you if that is the case.

How do I calculate the break-even point?

Add up the closing costs of the new loan. Then compare your new monthly principal and interest payment with your current one. Divide the costs by the monthly savings. If you plan to stay in the home well beyond that number of months, the refinance may make sense. Also consider the total interest over the life of each loan, not just the monthly payment.

Common reasons to refinance

  • Lower the monthly payment when market conditions or your credit have improved
  • Remove mortgage insurance after your home has gained value
  • Shorten the term to pay off the home sooner
  • Move from an adjustable rate to a fixed rate for stability
  • Access equity for renovations, debt consolidation or other goals

What should I know about cash-out refinancing in Texas?

Texas has some of the most specific home-equity rules in the country. A cash-out refinance of a homestead is governed by the Texas Constitution, which limits how much of the home’s value can be borrowed against, requires waiting periods before closing, sets rules about where the loan can close and limits how often you can do one. These rules protect homeowners, but they affect timing and options, so plan ahead.

If you only need a portion of your equity, a home equity loan or line of credit that leaves your first mortgage in place may be worth comparing.

When waiting is the better choice

If you are planning to move soon, if the savings are small relative to costs, or if your credit is about to improve, waiting may serve you better. A good loan officer will say so.
